Why Are Police Officers Called ‘Cops’?

To some they’re known as the fuzz, po-po, or five-0. But the most common term for police officers is “cops,” and here’s why.

by Bennett Kleinman
Share
Share to email Share to X Share to Pinterest Share link

The theme from the TV show Cops repeatedly asks, “Whatcha gonna do when they come for you?” But curious linguists may pose an entirely different question: Where does the word “cops” come from? While it’s common to refer to the police as cops, the terms don’t seem that similar, beyond a few shared letters. But there’s one backstory that explains the entire situation, as well as a few popular, albeit untrue theories we’d like to dispel.

One oft-repeated theory is that “cop” is an acronym for “constable on patrol,” but that’s not true. Another common misconception is that it references the copper badges and buttons worn on vintage New York City police uniforms — also false. The plain reality is that “cop” is derived from the dated verb “to cop,” defined as “to seize” or “to capture,” as one might nab a criminal.

The English verb “cop” is of northern British origin, coined in the early 18th century. It may come from the French term caper, meaning “to seize,” which itself comes from the Latin capere, meaning “to take.”

The term “copper” — as in a police officer — came about in 1838, used in the February 3 edition of Cockney Adventures: “Do it at vonce, else the coppers ‘ill come,’ said he of the short pipe.” This evolution of the verb “cop” to the noun “copper” was pretty straightforward. It follows the trajectory of how other verbs became nouns, such as “make” to “maker,” “pitch” to “pitcher,” and so on.

Advertisement

Within two decades, the noun “copper” was shortened to just “cop.” In other words, we went from the verb “cop” to the noun “copper,” and then back to “cop,” but this time as a noun. The slang made its way to the United States by the late 1850s, as people began referring to police officers as cops far and wide. 

Other informal and slang terms have similarly clear backstories. For example, “po-po” is a shortened form of “police” that repeats the first syllable, and “five-0” (the numeral 0, not the letter “O”) comes from the television series Hawaii Five-0. (The name comes from Hawaii being the 50th state.) But origin stories aren’t always so well documented; for instance, nobody knows why people refer to police as fuzz, and yet the slang persists.

What Is a Group of Peacocks Called?

You might have heard about “murders of crows” and “flamboyances of flamingos.” Those are just a few examples of the many terms — some of which are downright silly — used to describe groups of animals.

by Bennett Kleinman
Share
Share to email Share to X Share to Pinterest Share link

In 1486, the St. Albans Press in England published a work about heraldry, falconry, and hunting, titled the Boke of Seynt Albans — or Book of Saint Albans in modern spelling. Traditionally attributed to Dame Juliana Berners, the book is most famous for publicizing a list of collective nouns to denote certain animal groups. These collective nouns — known more specifically as terms of venery — have maintained lasting appeal, and some of them remain a part of our collective lexicon centuries later. We covered some of our favorites in a previous piece, but here are a few more that caught our eye.

Singular of Boars

It may seem oxymoronic to suggest that a group of boars could be described by the term “singular.” But in this case, the word is derived from the French sanglier, which refers to a full-grown wild boar. Adult boars do indeed have a reputation for living on their own, but that didn’t stop Dame Berners from co-opting the term to refer to groups of them.

Peep of Chickens


Marshmallow Peeps are a popular Easter confection. But the term “peep” can also refer to a group of actual chickens instead of ones made from sugar. The term is a reference to the sound that young chicks often make, which is known as peeping.

Leap of Leopards


The collective noun “leap of leopards” sounds perfectly pleasant rolling off the tongue. But this term of venery is more than just a playful case of alliteration. It references the incredible leaping ability of leopards, which can jump up to 10 feet in the air or 20 feet leaping ahead.

Murmuration of Starlings


The word “murmuration” has two definitions, one of which relates to low noises called murmurs. But in this case it refers to big flocks of birds, specifically starlings. A murmuration can consist of several hundred or even thousands of starlings, which can be seen soaring into the sky as if part of a giant wave.

Pace of Asses


Donkeys — or asses, as they’re sometimes known — are among the most reliable pack animals that exist. For this reason, a group of donkeys is called a pace, referring to the slow and steady nature of their gait as they tote materials around.

Muster of Peacocks


The word “muster” can be defined as “the act of assembling.” It’s commonly used when talking about military formations, though according to the Book of St. Albans, it can also be used to refer to groups of peacocks. An alternative term for a group of peacocks is “ostentation,” which is defined as “an excessive display” — much like a peacock’s set of showy feathers.

Labour of Moles


Long before the Book of Saint Albans was published, other authors wrote about the hardworking nature of moles. Take the Roman poet Virgil, who wrote of the animal, “For gather’d Grain the blind laborious Mole.” “Labour of moles” uses the British spelling of “labor.”

Dule of Turtledoves


The word “dule” (or “dole”) means “grief” or “sorrow.” In folklore, turtledoves — a bird native to parts of Eurasia — were thought to have a melancholy voice that evoked senses of sorrow in mourners. So it made sense to call a group of turtledoves a dule or dole, playing off their reputation.

Harass of Horses


This collective noun has nothing to do with horses going around harassing people who don’t provide apples. It comes from the Middle English haras, referring to an enclosure for herds of stud horses. That word comes from the Old French haraz, meaning “troop of horses.”

Dissimulation of Birds


If you’re dealing with birds but don’t quite know what type, you can refer to the group as a dissimulation. This term means “the act of dissembling” or “to hide under a false appearance.” Given the Book of St. Albans tackles the topic of hunting, it’s thought to come from the idea that birds fly in large packs to give off a false perception of being much larger, which might confuse predators and hunters.
